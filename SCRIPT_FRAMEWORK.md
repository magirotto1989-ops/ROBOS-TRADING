# Faceless Documentary Script Framework — WWII Channel

---

## OVERVIEW

A WWII documentary script must do 3 things simultaneously:
1. **Inform** — deliver accurate, well-sourced historical content
2. **Entertain** — maintain narrative tension like a thriller
3. **Retain** — prevent drop-off at every 30-second mark

For a faceless channel, the **script IS the show**. Great visuals can save a weak script — but a great script can carry weak visuals. Invest 70% of your production effort here.

---

## THE 8-PART DOCUMENTARY STRUCTURE

---

### PART 1: COLD OPEN (0:00 – 0:45) — "THE HOOK"

**Purpose:** Force the algorithm to keep watching. YouTube measures the first 30 seconds of retention as a quality signal.

**Formula:** Drop the viewer into the most dramatic moment of the story — BEFORE any context. Then pull back.

**What it must contain:**
- A specific, vivid scene (date, location, characters)
- A statement that contradicts conventional wisdom OR creates immediate tension
- An implied promise: "By the end of this video, you will understand something you've never understood before"

**Example Cold Open (Battle of Midway):**
> *"At 10:22 AM on June 4, 1942 — the entire course of World War II changed in exactly four minutes and thirty seconds. In that time, three Japanese aircraft carriers were transformed from the most powerful naval strike force in history into burning, sinking wreckage. What happened in those four minutes is not just one of history's most remarkable naval victories — it's a story of intelligence, luck, and one decision that Japan never recovered from."*

**Rules:**
- Never start with "Welcome to [Channel Name]" — this is a retention killer
- Never start with background context — earn the viewer's attention before explaining anything
- End the cold open with a direct-to-camera question OR a cliffhanger that forces them to stay

---

### PART 2: THE PROMISE (0:45 – 1:30) — "WHAT YOU'LL LEARN"

**Purpose:** Tell the viewer exactly what revelation they are about to receive. This functions as a verbal thumbnail.

**Formula:** "In this video, we're going to uncover [SPECIFIC REVELATION] — and explain why [MAINSTREAM UNDERSTANDING IS INCOMPLETE/WRONG]."

**Example:**
> *"In this video, we're going to go beyond the traditional account of Midway — past the 'American code-breaking and Japanese arrogance' narrative — to the exact sequence of decisions, accidents, and near-misses that made the outcome so extraordinarily unlikely. Because the truth is: the Americans nearly lost. And understanding why they didn't reveals something profound about how wars are actually decided."*

**Rules:**
- Explicitly state what the viewer will KNOW that they don't know now
- Use the word "actually" or "real" to signal a revelation is coming
- Keep it under 45 seconds

---

### PART 3: CONTEXT (1:30 – 3:30) — "THE STAGE"

**Purpose:** Give the viewer just enough background to understand the story. No more.

**What to cover:**
- Strategic situation before the event
- Key players (2–3 maximum — don't introduce 8 generals at once)
- The stakes: what happens if this battle is won/lost

**Narration Techniques:**
- Use present tense for immediacy: "It is June 1942. Japan controls..." not "In June 1942, Japan controlled..."
- Use specific numbers: "850 aircraft" not "a massive fleet"
- Name individuals with their role: "Vice Admiral Chuichi Nagumo, commander of Japan's First Carrier Strike Force"

**Visual cue for editor:** This is where you use animated maps, showing the strategic situation before the battle begins.

---

### PART 4: THE BUILD (3:30 – 6:30) — "THE TENSION"

**Purpose:** Walk through the events chronologically, but frame each development as a decision under pressure.

**The core narration rhythm:**
1. What was the situation at this moment?
2. What decision did the commander face?
3. What information did they have — and what did they NOT know?
4. What did they decide?
5. What were the immediate consequences?

**This rhythm prevents passive documentary narration** ("And then X happened, and then Y happened") and transforms your script into a series of tension-decision-consequence cycles that keep viewers engaged.

**Example (Battle of Midway):**
> *"At 7:28 AM, Nagumo received a report that American ships had been spotted. But crucially — his scout plane had failed to identify what type of ships. Were they destroyers? Cruisers? Carriers?*
>
> *Nagumo now faced an agonizing choice. His second wave of aircraft — which had been armed with torpedoes to attack ships — were being re-armed with bombs for a second land strike on Midway. Should he reverse course, re-arm them for anti-ship combat, and wait?*
>
> *He chose to wait. And in that decision, the fate of the Imperial Japanese Navy was sealed."*

---

### PART 5: THE MID-ROLL RETENTION HOOK (6:00 – 6:30) — "THE RE-HOOK"

**Purpose:** Prevent the drop-off that typically occurs at the 6–7 minute mark. This is when YouTube shows ads and many viewers leave.

**Formula:** At the 5:30–6:00 mark, insert a "coming up" tease that makes leaving feel like a loss.

**Example:**
> *"In just a moment, we're going to look at the four-minute sequence that sank three Japanese carriers — and at the one American pilot whose completely accidental discovery set it all in motion. Stay with us."*

**Rules:**
- Never say "Don't forget to like and subscribe" at the mid-roll — it signals the creator is insecure and interrupts the narrative
- The re-hook should feel like a natural part of the narration, not a YouTube meta-comment

---

### PART 6: THE CLIMAX (6:30 – 10:00) — "THE TURNING POINT"

**Purpose:** Deliver the payoff of the narrative — the most dramatic moment, the key revelation, the critical decision's consequence.

**Techniques:**
- Slow down time — describe key moments second by second
- Use direct quotes from historical figures (sourced)
- Use contrast: "Meanwhile, 30 miles away, the Japanese had no idea..."
- Use numbers to create scale: "Of the 41 Devastator torpedo bombers that attacked, 35 were shot down. Not a single torpedo hit."

**Example:**
> *"At 10:22 AM, Lieutenant Commander McClusky led his dive bombers in. The Japanese carriers were caught completely unprepared — decks crowded with aircraft being re-fueled, re-armed. The Akagi was hit by three bombs. The Kaga by four. The Soryu by three.*
>
> *Within six minutes, three of Japan's six fleet carriers — the ships that had attacked Pearl Harbor — were dying.*
>
> *Nagumo transferred his flag to a cruiser. He was reportedly so stunned that aides had to physically restrain him from going down with his ship."*

---

### PART 7: THE AFTERMATH & LEGACY (10:00 – 12:30) — "THE CONSEQUENCES"

**Purpose:** Answer "so what?" — why did this matter beyond the battle itself?

**What to cover:**
- Immediate strategic consequences
- Long-term war impact
- Human cost (specific numbers, specific stories)
- The historical verdict — how do modern historians assess this event?

**The "Zoom Out" technique:** End the consequences section by connecting the specific battle to the broader arc of WWII or human history:
> *"Midway did not end the Pacific War. But it fundamentally changed its character. Japan lost four carriers and their best pilots. They would never fully replace them. The initiative in the Pacific passed permanently to the Americans."*

---

### PART 8: THE CLOSING HOOK (12:30 – 13:30) — "THE SUBSCRIBE BAIT"

**Purpose:** Convert viewers into subscribers by creating anticipation for the next video.

**Formula:**
1. Summarize the revelation you just delivered (30 seconds)
2. Connect it to a larger truth about WWII/warfare/human nature (15 seconds)
3. Tease the next video with a cliffhanger (30 seconds)
4. Subscribe CTA — brief and earned (15 seconds)

**Example:**
> *"The Battle of Midway was decided not by superior firepower, but by a chain of intelligence advantages, Japanese overconfidence, and — critically — four minutes of extraordinarily fortunate timing.*
>
> *It's a reminder that in war, as in most human endeavors, the difference between victory and catastrophe often comes down to a single decision made with incomplete information under extreme pressure.*
>
> *Next week, we're looking at the man whose decision at Midway arguably saved the Pacific War — and who then made a catastrophic error that almost gave it back to Japan. Subscribe so you don't miss it.*
>
> *I'll see you in the next one."*

**Rules:**
- Never end with "If you enjoyed this video, please like and subscribe" — it's weak
- Always tease the next video by title or topic
- Make the subscribe CTA feel like an invitation to continue a conversation, not a transaction

## WORD COUNT GUIDE

| Video Length | Target Word Count | Reading Speed |
|---|---|---|
| 8 minutes | 1,000–1,200 words | ~130 wpm (documentary pace) |
| 10 minutes | 1,300–1,500 words | ~130 wpm |
| 12 minutes | 1,600–1,900 words | ~130 wpm |
| 15 minutes | 2,000–2,400 words | ~130 wpm |

**Pro tip:** Speak slower than you think is natural. Documentary narrators speak at 120–140 wpm. Podcasters speak at 150–170 wpm. The pace signals gravitas.

## NARRATION STYLE GUIDE

### Sentence Structure
- Use short, declarative sentences for dramatic moments: "He was wrong."
- Use longer sentences for context and buildup
- Vary sentence length deliberately — monotone sentence length = monotone energy

### Vocabulary
- Prefer concrete nouns over abstract ones: "850 aircraft" not "significant airpower"
- Prefer active voice: "Hitler ordered..." not "An order was given by Hitler..."
- Use military terminology correctly — the audience will notice errors
- Avoid academic hedging: "may have possibly contributed" — commit to your analysis

### Tension Language Bank
Use these words and phrases to maintain narrative energy:
```
Meanwhile... | But then... | What [X] didn't know... | In that moment...
The decision that followed... | By the time [X] realized... | What happened next...
This was the turning point... | Everything changed when... | No one could have predicted...
The cost would be catastrophic... | [X] had [N] hours left... | For the last time...
```

## RESEARCH CHECKLIST (Pre-Script)

Before writing any script, complete:

- [ ] Read at least one scholarly/military history book on the topic
- [ ] Cross-reference dates and names across 2+ sources
- [ ] Find 2–3 direct quotes from participants (letters, memoirs, orders)
- [ ] Identify 1 "surprising fact" that contradicts popular understanding
- [ ] Identify the single most dramatic moment — this is your cold open
- [ ] Note exact casualty figures from reliable sources (avoid Wikipedia as primary)
- [ ] Confirm spelling of all proper names and place names
- [ ] Source all archival footage before finalizing script (ensures B-roll exists)

## VOICEOVER PRODUCTION NOTES

### AI Voice (ElevenLabs)
- **Recommended voices:** "Daniel" (authoritative male), "Antoni" (warm, cinematic)
- **Speed setting:** 0.92–0.95 (slightly slower than natural)
- **Stability:** 0.70–0.80 (slight variation = more natural)
- **Insert pauses:** Use `[pause]` tags or line breaks at dramatic moments
- **Export format:** WAV 44.1kHz for editing flexibility

### Human Voiceover (Recommended for scale)
- Brief: "Military documentary style, authoritative but not robotic. Think Ken Burns + military historian."
- Record takes slowly — easier to speed up than to pitch-correct rushed takes
- Separate takes for each section (easier to re-record individual parts)
